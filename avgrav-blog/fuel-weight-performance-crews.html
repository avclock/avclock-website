Why Crews Care About Every Pound of Fuel

4 min read

When a crew orders fuel in pounds or asks what the uplift weighed, it isn't small talk. That number goes into the crew's own planning. You don't need to know how they do their job to do yours well, but it helps to know why the number matters.

This post is from the fueler's side. Weight and balance, performance, and fuel planning are the crew's work, done with the airplane's own data and their procedures.

Fuel Is Weight

Everything on board counts toward the airplane's weight, and fuel is often a big part of it. The FAA's Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge notes that fuel burn is normally the only weight change during a flight, and that as fuel is used, the airplane gets lighter and its performance improves. So the crew cares about the weight at the start, and how it changes on the way.

Fuel Moves the Balance

The same handbook notes that burning fuel can also move the center of gravity, depending on where the tanks are. That's one reason crews care which tanks get the fuel and how much goes in each. If they ask for a particular split between tanks, that's why. Fuel exactly the way it was ordered, and ask before changing anything.

The Plan Depends on the Load

Crews plan a flight around a fuel load. Too little, and they may not have what they planned for. Too much, and the extra weight may cost them somewhere else. That's why an order like "total 8,000 pounds" means exactly that, and why you confirm total versus add, and pounds versus gallons, before you start. The pound orders guide covers the questions.

Not Every Gallon Weighs the Same

A gallon of Jet A doesn't have one fixed weight. It depends on the fuel's density, which varies from batch to batch, and on its temperature. Using AvGrav's correction math, here's 1,000 gallons at 60°F at three gravities inside the typical Jet A range:

API @ 60°Flb/gal1,000 gal
38.06.9526,952 lb
43.796.7226,722 lb
48.06.5656,565 lb

That's almost 400 pounds between the heaviest and the lightest, for the same gallons. Temperature adds to it: 1,000 gallons of the 43.79 API fuel metered at 30°F is 1,015.2 gallons at 60°F, about 6,824 pounds, not 6,722. The gross vs. net gallons post explains the correction.

What That Means at the Truck

  • Use the fuel's real gravity when you convert pounds to gallons, or gallons to pounds, not a rule of thumb.
  • Correct for temperature unless your meter already does.
  • Label everything on the ticket: gallons or liters, pounds or kilograms, and whether a volume is metered or corrected.
  • Tell the crew right away if what went in isn't what they ordered. A surprise later is worse than a short delay now.

The density uplift check post covers how crews may compare your number with their gauges.

When You're Not Sure

If you can't tell whether the crew wants pounds or gallons, total or add, or which tanks, ask before the nozzle goes in. Crews would rather answer one more question than fix a fuel load after the fact. If the gravity reading looks odd for the grade, stop and check it before you use it in any conversion.

Common Questions

Why do pilots care about fuel weight?

Fuel is part of the airplane's weight, and weight affects performance and balance. The FAA's Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge notes that fuel burn is normally the only weight change during a flight, and that it can move the center of gravity depending on where the tanks are.

Is 6.7 pounds per gallon accurate for Jet A?

It's a common approximation, but the real weight depends on the fuel's density and temperature. Using AvGrav's math, Jet A at 38 API weighs 6.952 pounds per gallon at 60°F and Jet A at 48 API weighs 6.565, a difference of almost 400 pounds over 1,000 gallons.

What should line service give the crew?

Whatever the crew asks for, clearly labeled: the grade, the quantity and its units, and the weight if they want it, worked out from the gravity of the fuel you pumped.
