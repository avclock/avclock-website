Marshalling Basics: Clear Signals and When to Stop

4 min read

Marshalling looks simple from a distance: someone with wands guides an aircraft into its spot. Up close, it's a job where the pilot is trusting you to see what they can't, especially around wingtips and tails. Done well, it's quiet and routine. Done badly, it can end with an aircraft touching something on the ramp.

Your FBO's training and procedures, and the official signals, are what you follow. This post covers the basics that run through all of them.

Training Comes First

Nobody should marshal an aircraft without training and sign-off under your FBO's procedures. That includes the signals, where to stand, how to work with wing walkers, and what to do when something goes wrong. Practice with a trainer before you do it for real. The new line tech checklist is a good place to track it.

Use the Standard Signals

In the U.S., the FAA's Aeronautical Information Manual includes standard ground hand signals between a signalman and a pilot. Learn them from that source and your training. Standard signals matter because the pilot may have never been to your airport and has no idea how your FBO usually does things. A made-up or sloppy signal means the pilot has to guess.

  • Make each signal clearly and hold it long enough to be seen.
  • Don't improvise. If a situation isn't covered by the signals you know, stop the aircraft and sort it out.

Know the Spot Before It Arrives

  • Walk the area for FOD, equipment, and anything near where the wings and tail will be.
  • Know where the aircraft needs to stop, and leave room for fuel trucks, ground power, and the crew's doors.
  • Have chocks ready before the aircraft gets there.

The quick turn post covers being ready when the airplane arrives.

Stand Where the Pilot Can See You

Stand where the pilot can see you clearly from the cockpit and where you can see the aircraft and what's around it. Stay clear of the engine and propeller danger areas and the path of the aircraft. If you lose sight of the pilot, or think they've lost sight of you, stop the aircraft.

Wing Walkers

In tight spots, one marshaller can't see every wingtip and the tail at once. Use wing walkers the way your procedures say, and agree on how they'll signal you before the aircraft moves. The towing and wing walkers post covers working together.

At Night

The signals don't change after dark, but the pilot has to be able to see them, so signalers commonly use lighted wands. Wear your high-visibility gear, check your wands before the aircraft arrives, and give yourself more time. The night ramp post covers the rest.

When in Doubt, Stop

The most important signal is the stop. Use it whenever you can't see the clearance, a person or vehicle moves into the path, a wing walker signals you, or something just doesn't look right. A stopped aircraft can always move again. A wingtip that touched something can't be undone. Then, once it's stopped, sort out the problem before you signal it forward again.

Common Questions

Where do aircraft marshalling signals come from?

In the U.S., the FAA's Aeronautical Information Manual includes standard ground hand signals between a signalman and a pilot. Learn them from that source and your FBO's training, not from memory or videos.

Who is allowed to marshal aircraft?

Whoever your FBO has trained and signed off to do it. Marshalling an aircraft without training puts the aircraft, the people around it, and you at risk.

What should a marshaller do if they lose sight of a wingtip?

Stop the aircraft. If you can't see the clearance, or you're not sure, the stop signal is always the right call.
