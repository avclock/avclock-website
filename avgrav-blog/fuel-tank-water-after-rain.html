After Heavy Rain: Checking Tanks and Trucks for Water

4 min read

Most days, the daily sump is routine. After a heavy storm, it's worth more attention. Rain looks for any opening that doesn't seal well, and fuel systems have plenty of caps, hatches, and seals that age over time. The checks are the same ones you do every day. The difference is looking a little harder.

Your FBO's procedures and your quality program set the actual steps. The water in jet fuel post covers what water looks like and why it matters. This post is about storms.

Where Rain Gets In

FAA guidance on airport fuel systems lists some of the ways water enters: leaks in underground tanks, and leaky seals on dome covers, floating roofs, and hatches during rain or snow storms. On aircraft, Australia's Civil Aviation Safety Authority has noted that water contamination problems in small piston aircraft tend to follow heavy rain, traced to poorly fitting or unapproved caps, failed seals, and damaged filler necks. The common thread is anything that's supposed to seal and doesn't.

At the Fuel Farm

  • Sump the tanks and filters the way your procedures say, and look closely at what comes out. Keep going as your procedures direct if the first sample isn't clean.
  • Look at hatches, fill caps, and vents for damage, missing seals, or anything that could let water in.
  • Check spill buckets and containment for rainwater. Remove it the way your procedures and environmental rules say, and never drain it into the tank.
  • Watch the tank levels. A level that rises, or doesn't drop the way it should, is worth a closer look at the sump.

The fuel farm daily check covers the full walkthrough.

On the Trucks

  • Sump each truck before it goes back in service, and check the sample carefully.
  • Look at the dome cover and its seal, the fill caps, and the vents.
  • Check filter sumps the way your procedures say.

The fuel truck daily check covers the rest.

Self-Serve and Aircraft

If your FBO has a self-serve pump, give it the same attention: sumps, caps, and the hose and nozzle. For aircraft parked outside through a storm, the pilot's preflight sump is their job, but if you see a cap that looks loose, damaged, or wrong for the airplane, tell the crew.

When You Find Water

Finding some water in a sump is what sumps are for. Finding more than usual, or water that keeps coming, is a reason to stop and tell your supervisor before the fuel goes anywhere. Follow your procedures on taking the tank or truck out of service and finding the source.

Write It Down

Record what you checked and what you found, and note the storm. A log that says "heavy rain overnight, extra sumps, all clear" is useful the next time, and it shows how a tank or truck behaves in bad weather. A pattern of water after every storm points to a seal or cap that needs fixing.

Common Questions

How does rainwater get into fuel tanks?

Through any opening that doesn't seal properly. FAA guidance on airport fuel systems lists leaks in underground tanks and leaky seals on dome covers, floating roofs, and hatches during rain or snow storms. On aircraft, Australia's aviation safety regulator has traced water to poorly fitting caps, failed seals, and damaged filler necks.

What should line service check after heavy rain?

Follow your procedures. The usual extra attention goes to tank and filter sumps, truck sumps, vents, fill caps, hatches and seals, and spill containment, with a record of what you found.

Should rainwater in a spill bucket be drained into the tank?

No. Water collected in a spill bucket or containment should be removed the way your procedures and environmental rules say, never drained into the fuel tank.
